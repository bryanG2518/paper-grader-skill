# MBA case analysis rubric

Use for case studies, market entry analyses, strategy memos, competitive analyses,
and consulting-style deliverables. Graduate business grading rewards a defensible
recommendation over a thorough description. A paper can cover everything and still
earn a B by never committing to a course of action.

| Category | Weight | What full marks look like |
|---|---|---|
| Problem definition | 15% | Names the actual decision to be made, not the symptoms; distinguishes the root issue from its consequences |
| Analysis and frameworks | 25% | Frameworks are applied to the case's facts and produce a conclusion, not filled in as an exercise |
| Evidence and quantification | 20% | Financials, market sizing, or unit economics support the argument; assumptions are stated and sourced |
| Recommendation | 25% | One clear course of action, with implementation steps, resourcing, timeline, and success measures |
| Risk and alternatives | 10% | Options considered and rejected with reasons; key risks named with mitigations |
| Structure and professionalism | 5% | Reads as a document an executive would act on; exhibits labeled and referenced in the text |

## Category tests

**Problem definition:** if the problem statement could be lifted into any other case
in the same industry, it's too generic. It should be unresolvable without this case's
specific facts.

**Frameworks:** a SWOT, Porter's Five Forces, or PESTEL that appears as a filled-in
grid and is never referenced again is decoration. Every framework must end with a
sentence beginning "therefore": it either changes the recommendation or it comes out.

**Quantification:** graduate business work is expected to carry numbers. Market size,
revenue impact, cost structure, payback period, or breakeven. At minimum one number
should be essential to the recommendation. Every assumption behind a number needs
a source or a stated basis.

**Recommendation:** test it against these five questions:
1. Could a reader act on this Monday morning?
2. Does it say who does what, by when?
3. What does it cost, and where does the money come from?
4. How will they know if it worked?
5. Does it follow from the analysis, or could it have been written without it?

Any "no" is a finding.

**Risk:** naming risks without mitigations reads as a hedge. Each named risk needs a
response, and at least one should be a risk that could actually sink the plan, not a
generic one like "market conditions may change."

## Common point losses

1. **Analysis with no decision:** thorough research, then a recommendation section that lists options without choosing.
2. **Framework theater:** frameworks applied because they were taught, not because they resolve anything in this case.
3. **Numbers with no provenance:** a market size figure with no source, or a projection with no stated assumptions.
4. **Ignoring the constraints the case gives:** recommending an approach the case explicitly rules out on capital, regulatory, or timeline grounds.
5. **No implementation:** the recommendation ends at "expand into the market" with no first ninety days.
6. **Exhibits that float:** charts and tables that appear in an appendix and are never cited in the body.
