# Arts & Humanities

## How this professor reads

The argument is the contribution. There is no dataset to hide behind, so a paper
lives or dies on whether it makes a claim someone could disagree with and then
defends it against the best objection.

Shared standards across the area:

- **Primary sources do the work.** A paper that engages the text, object, site, or
  score directly outranks one that summarizes what critics have said about it.
  Secondary literature situates the argument; it doesn't substitute for it.
- **Close reading over summary:** plot recap, historical narration, and description
  of a building are setup, not analysis. If a reader who knows the source learns
  nothing, the paper hasn't started yet.
- **Quotation is evidence and must be interpreted.** A quote dropped in and left
  unanalyzed is a dangling assertion. Every quotation needs a sentence after it
  saying what to notice.
- **Situate the claim in the scholarship.** Graduate work should show awareness of
  the existing conversation and say what it adds to it.
- **Anachronism is a serious error.** Reading present-day categories into a past
  text or culture without acknowledging the move costs points.
- **Prose quality is graded.** In this area, style is not a polish item. Muddy
  sentences are treated as muddy thinking.

**Typical weight shift** from `rubrics/default-academic.md`: raise Thesis and
argument to 30%, raise Evidence and analysis to 30%, cut Structure to 10%.

### Architecture and Built Environment
- **Reads for:** an argument about how form, program, site, and inhabitation relate, rather than a building described admiringly.
- **Evidence:** plans, sections, site analysis, precedent studies, and drawings referenced in the text. Images that appear without being discussed are decoration.
- **Citation:** Chicago or Harvard; every drawing and photograph needs a credit.
- **Loses points for:** calling a building "iconic" or "innovative" without saying what it does differently; ignoring climate, code, cost, or how people actually move through the space.

### Art and Design
- **Reads for:** visual analysis that earns its interpretation: composition, material, and technique tied to meaning and context.
- **Evidence:** the work itself, described precisely, plus period context and critical reception. Figure numbers cited in the body.
- **Citation:** Chicago; full captions with artist, title, date, medium, dimensions, collection.
- **Loses points for:** biography substituting for analysis; asserting what a work "expresses" with nothing in the work supporting it.

### Archaeology
- **Reads for:** interpretation properly constrained by what the material evidence can actually support.
- **Evidence:** stratigraphy, assemblage data, dating methods with their error ranges, and site reports. Uncertainty stated, not smoothed over.
- **Citation:** Harvard or SAA style; sites, contexts, and accession numbers specified.
- **Loses points for:** overreading fragmentary evidence; ignoring taphonomy and sampling bias; treating one site as representative of a culture.

### Classics and Ancient History
- **Reads for:** engagement with the ancient text or source in its own terms, and awareness of how it came down to us.
- **Evidence:** primary sources cited by standard reference (book, chapter, line), with translation choices acknowledged where they carry weight.
- **Citation:** Chicago with standard abbreviations; name the edition and translator.
- **Loses points for:** treating ancient authors as neutral reporters; relying only on translations where the argument turns on a specific word; ignoring the source's genre and audience.

### English Language and Literature
- **Reads for:** a reading of the text that is arguable, specific, and supported line by line.
- **Evidence:** the text itself, quoted closely and analyzed. Critical sources engaged, not just cited approvingly.
- **Citation:** MLA 9; parenthetical page or line numbers on every quotation.
- **Loses points for:** plot summary; "the author is saying that…"; theory applied as a label rather than a tool; a thesis nobody could dispute.

### History
- **Reads for:** an argument grounded in primary sources, alert to change over time and to what the sources can and cannot tell us.
- **Evidence:** archival or primary material, read critically: who made this source, for whom, and why. Historiography engaged where the debate matters.
- **Citation:** Chicago notes and bibliography. Footnotes, not parentheticals.
- **Loses points for:** narrating events instead of arguing about them; presentism; using secondary works as if they were primary evidence; a thesis that is only a topic.

### Linguistics
- **Reads for:** a claim about language tested against data, with the analysis stated formally enough to be wrong.
- **Evidence:** numbered examples with glosses and grammaticality judgments; corpus data with source and size; counterexamples addressed rather than omitted.
- **Citation:** Unified Style Sheet for Linguistics or APA; standard Leipzig glossing.
- **Loses points for:** examples that don't support the rule as stated; ignoring the obvious counterexample; conflating description with prescription.

### Modern Languages
- **Reads for:** engagement with the text in the original language, and cultural context handled with specificity.
- **Evidence:** original-language quotation with translation where required by the course, and attention to what the original does that translation loses.
- **Citation:** MLA 9; state whose translation is used, or mark it as your own.
- **Loses points for:** arguing about the text while quoting only translation; national generalizations offered as context; grammatical errors in the target language, which are graded here.

### Performing Arts
- **Reads for:** analysis of performance as performance: embodiment, staging, time, and audience, not just the script or score.
- **Evidence:** specific productions, performances, or recordings, identified by date, venue, and personnel. Score or text excerpts where the argument turns on them.
- **Citation:** Chicago or MLA; full performance details.
- **Loses points for:** treating a play as literature and never addressing staging; evaluative reviewing in place of analysis; describing a performance nobody can locate.

### Philosophy
- **Reads for:** a valid argument, stated clearly, with the strongest objection raised and answered.
- **Evidence:** argument itself. Premises made explicit, terms defined on first use, thought experiments used to isolate one variable.
- **Citation:** Chicago or APA; cite by standard pagination for canonical texts.
- **Loses points for:** appeal to authority instead of argument; missing objection sections; equivocation on a key term; summarizing three philosophers and never taking a position.

### Theology and Religious Studies
- **Reads for:** careful handling of texts and traditions, with the paper's own methodological stance made clear.
- **Evidence:** scripture and tradition cited by standard reference; attention to genre, canon, and translation; secondary scholarship engaged critically.
- **Citation:** SBL Handbook or Chicago.
- **Loses points for:** confessional assertion presented as argument in a religious studies course, or the reverse when the course is confessional; proof-texting; describing a tradition from outside without acknowledging the vantage point.
